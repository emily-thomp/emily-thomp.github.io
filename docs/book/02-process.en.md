# Nitric acid by ammonia oxidation

## Principles

Catalytic oxidation of an ammonia–air mixture over platinum or a platinum–rhodium alloy first produces nitric oxide. Further oxidation produces nitrogen dioxide, which is absorbed in water to make nitric acid. This established method has two stages.

Oxidation of ammonia over the catalyst:

$$
4\mathrm{NH_3}+5\mathrm{O_1}=4\mathrm{NO}+6\mathrm{H_2O}+216{,}700\,\text{cal}
$$

Oxidation of nitric oxide and absorption in water:

$$
2\mathrm{NO}+\mathrm{O_2}=2\mathrm{NO_2}+29{,}500\,\text{cal}
$$

$$
3\mathrm{NO_2}+\mathrm{H_2O}=2\mathrm{HNO_3}+\mathrm{NO}+17{,}590\,\text{cal}
$$

> Editorial note: the oxygen subscript in the first printed equation resembles “1”; that reading is retained. The usual chemical species is $\mathrm{O_2}$. The original diagram also connects the NO produced in the third reaction back to the second.

Previously, this process generally used large conventional plants. Platinum–rhodium gauze was expensive, the ammonia contact reactor was made of high-chromium steel, and absorption towers, blowers, acid pumps and pipework required considerable stainless steel. Limited domestic stainless-steel supplies hindered wider adoption. The locally equipped method aimed to overcome these material shortages by using inexpensive local materials and a simpler flowsheet, reducing investment and making operation easier to establish widely.

## Production flowsheet

Ammonia in cylinders is vaporized by hot water or steam and admitted to the pressure-buffer vessel (1). A valve controls its flow, which is measured with an orifice flowmeter (2), before it enters the ammonia–air mixer (4).

| 3 | Air filter |
| 4 | Ammonia–air mixer |
| 5 | Ammonia oxidizer |
| 6 | Blower |
| 7 | Heat exchanger |
| 8 | Oxidation tower |
| 9 | Acid absorption tower |
| 10 | Alkaline absorption tower |
| 11 | Pump |
| 12 | Cooler |

Air is filtered in (3) to remove dust and impurities and delivered by blower (6). A valve at the blower outlet controls air flow, measured by flowmeter (2). One portion passes through heat exchanger (7), where hot oxidizer outlet gas heats it; another goes directly to the mixer. The gases mix thoroughly before entering oxidizer (5).

The reactor contains an iron–chromium or iron–bismuth oxide catalyst, either alone or combined with a single platinum gauze. The reported oxidation efficiencies are 85–90% for the former arrangement and up to 96% for the combined bed. Ammonia is oxidized to nitric oxide, releasing substantial reaction heat. The hot nitrogen-oxide gas heats incoming air in exchanger (7). An additional hot-water exchanger can recover heat for vaporizing ammonia in the cylinders.

After exchanger (7), gas enters cooler (12), then oxidation tower (8), where nitric oxide oxidizes to nitrogen dioxide. A branch from the blower outlet supplies a little secondary air to the tower to provide sufficient oxygen.

Gas then enters five absorption towers (9). Water is added to the fifth. Each tower has a pump (11) circulating acid to absorb nitrogen dioxide. The weak acid formed in the fifth tower flows successively to the fourth and then towards the first, driven by differences in liquid level. Product acid leaves the first tower at a concentration of 45% or more.

Absorption releases heat, so the circulating acid requires thorough cooling. Acid coolers are generally installed at each pump inlet or outlet. Some nitrogen oxides remain in the gas leaving the fifth tower; an optional final alkaline tower absorbs them and produces some sodium nitrite solution.

## Construction and function of individual units

The following dimensions are based on production of approximately 1.5 tonnes per day of nitric acid on a 100% basis. They may be adjusted for other outputs.

### 1. Air filter

Air for ammonia oxidation must be clean. Contaminants can poison the catalyst, lower its activity or render it ineffective. Filtration removes dust, but not harmful gases such as hydrogen sulfide or acetylene; these must be prevented from entering the air supply.

The filter may be a wooden barrel or a converted diesel drum, about 0.8 m high and 0.4 m in diameter, packed with 20 × 20 mm ceramic rings. Short bamboo rings may substitute for ceramic rings. Wash and dry the packing, wet it with viscous machine oil, let excess oil drain, and place it in the barrel. Towelling or flannel at the bottom removes coarse dust; the oiled rings retain finer particles.

![Air filter](../assets/figures/p09_f01.png)

Replace and wash the cloth frequently. After several months, clean the rings as their condition requires: remove them, wash off deposits with kerosene, and reapply machine oil.

### 2. Air blower

The specified blower supplies about 350–400 m³/h at a pressure of 1.5–2.0 m water column, sufficient to drive gas through the system. A Roots blower normally meets this requirement. Prevent bearing oil from leaking into its gas chamber, since entrained oil adversely affects the catalyst.

A conventional centrifugal blower with about 500 mm water-column pressure is insufficient by itself. The book adds an exhaust fan of the same capacity after acid absorption tower (9) or alkaline tower (10). Coat this downstream fan with an acid-resistant material, such as perchlorovinyl varnish, against nitrogen-oxide corrosion.

The simple air-demand calculation uses 330–340 kg of ammonia for the stated production of 100% nitric acid. Hourly ammonia flow is:

$$
\frac{340\times22.4}{17\times24}=18.7\ \mathrm{m^3}
$$

For an ammonia concentration of 9–10% in the mixture, the calculation at 10% is:

$$
\frac{18.7}{18.7+X}=10\%
$$

This gives an air flow of 168.3 m³/h. A low-pressure centrifugal blower needs a somewhat greater rated capacity because downstream resistance reduces delivery.

### 3. Ammonia and air flowmeters

Both flows must be controlled strictly and kept stable, usually at 9–10.5% ammonia by volume. Large fluctuations disturb production. Suitable instruments include rotameters and differential-pressure flowmeters. Maximum capacities are about 40 m³/h for ammonia and 400 m³/h for air.

Where instruments are difficult to obtain, a simple orifice differential-pressure meter can be made more cheaply. A thin metal plate with a small central hole is clamped between flanges. Static-pressure taps upstream and downstream connect to an inclined liquid-column differential manometer, from which flow is determined.

![Orifice differential-pressure flowmeter](../assets/figures/p11_f01.png)

Calibrate it against an accurate meter before use. Connect a blower upstream and a gas meter downstream. Record the actual flow corresponding to different pressure differences, establishing a relation between manometer reading and m³/h.

### 4. Ammonia–air mixer

Thorough mixing ensures uniform reaction across the catalyst. Poor mixing gives locally high and low ammonia concentrations and lowers conversion. The book describes two simple designs.

#### Packed mixer

The aluminium mixer has a 100 mm air inlet and a 30 mm ammonia inlet. It contains 20 × 20 mm ceramic packing rings. Ammonia enters through a 10 mm nozzle, mixes with air at the nozzle outlet, and is mixed further in the packing before leaving for the oxidizer.

![Packed mixer](../assets/figures/p12_f01.png)

#### Baffled mixer

An aluminium vessel, 200 mm in diameter and about 1 m long, contains ten baffles. Repeated changes in gas direction produce mixing. Insulate its wall against heat loss; the original describes asbestos rope covered with an asbestos-based insulating paste.

![Baffled mixer](../assets/figures/p12_f02.png)

### 5. Catalytic ammonia oxidizer

This is a principal unit of the plant. Ammonia and air react over the catalyst to form nitrogen oxides and water vapour. The reaction releases considerable heat, requiring heat-resistant construction. Conventional reactors use high-chromium steel; the design described here uses refractory brick.

The mixture enters through a 100 mm aluminium pipe at the top. The aluminium cover is conical, at 45° to the horizontal, or preferably 65°, to distribute gas uniformly across the catalyst. An asbestos seal between cover and vessel is bolted tight. Two 100 mm viewing ports, covered with transparent mica, permit observation and also serve as ignition openings when the mica is removed.

![Oxidizer for a non-platinum catalyst](../assets/figures/p13_f01.png)

The iron shell can be assembled from old diesel drums. It has about 12 cm of insulating brick lining and an inner refractory-brick lining. A flange ring 30 cm below the top supports an optional platinum gauze; omit it if using only a non-platinum catalyst. A nickel mesh 50 cm below the top supports the catalyst. Stainless mesh with 16 openings per inch may substitute, or a perforated refractory plate with holes smaller than the catalyst particles and enough total open area to avoid excessive resistance.

A chromium–aluminium thermocouple is installed in the bed to measure temperature. Below 80 cm from the top, the vessel narrows into a cone connected to an iron outlet pipe; a 60° cone is preferable. Uniform gas distribution matters: uneven reaction can allow unreacted ammonia to pass through, forming white ammonium-nitrate mist in the product gas.

The viewing ports should expose the whole bed surface and allow the ignition flame to reach it. Seal the thermocouple penetration against leakage with matching flanges on the shell and thermocouple tube, asbestos packing, and bolts. Position its tip near the centre of the vessel.

![Thermocouple installation](../assets/figures/p14_f01.png)

Refractory-brick reactors may leak ammonia through cracks, bypassing the catalyst and reducing conversion while forming ammonium-nitrate mist. Weld a plate flange ring at the bed support so leaked gas still passes through the catalyst. Minimize thermal cracking, use relatively thick refractory mortar, and add a little water glass when mixing it.

#### Reactor dimensions and catalyst loading

The Shanghai Chemical Industry Research Institute reported the following test results:

| Quantity | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Reactor diameter | 150 mm |
| Cross-sectional area | 0.0177 m² |
| Iron–bismuth bed height | 60 mm |
| Ammonia concentration | 10.5% |
| Reaction temperature | 720–750°C |
| Ammonia processed | 15 kg/day |
| Oxidation efficiency | About 90% |

For 500 kg of ammonia per day, the calculated area is:

$$
\frac{500}{15}\times0.0177=0.59\ \mathrm{m^2}
$$

The corresponding diameter follows from:

$$
\frac{\pi D^2}{4}=0.59,\qquad D=0.87\ \mathrm{m}
$$

The stated catalyst requirement is:

$$
0.59\times0.08\times1.3=0.061\ \text{tonnes}
$$

Load the bed to about 60–80 mm and spread the randomly packed catalyst evenly. The institute's iron–bismuth catalyst consists of discs about 7 mm in diameter and 3 mm thick.

With one platinum gauze combined with iron–bismuth catalyst, the reported results are a 50 mm catalyst bed, 10 mm separation between gauze and catalyst, ammonia throughput of 3,000–3,200 kg per square metre per day, concentration of 10.0–10.5%, and efficiency of 95–97%. Efficiency remained above 95% after three months.

The combined arrangement permits a smaller diameter. At 500 kg/day:

$$
\frac{500\ \mathrm{kg}\times1\ \mathrm{m^2}}{3000\ \mathrm{kg}}=0.17\ \mathrm{m^2}
$$

$$
\frac{\pi D^2}{4}=0.17,\qquad D=465\ \mathrm{mm}
$$

The institute also designed a small sodium-nitrite plant with one platinum gauze and iron–bismuth catalyst. A 520 mm reactor processed about 660 kg of ammonia daily, with the nitrogen oxides absorbed in alkaline solution in a spray tower to produce about 2,000–2,200 kg of sodium nitrite. Huayuan Dye Works, China No. 3 Dye Works, Wuzhou Pharmaceutical Works and Xinfeng Textile Printing Works had used this design.

Non-platinum iron–bismuth reactors were used by the institute, Tiangong Acid and Alkali Works and Dafeng Chemical Works. Throughput varied with diameter but broadly agreed with these calculations. Reducing diameter and compensating by making the catalyst bed deeper made operation difficult.

### 6. Air preheater (heat exchanger)

Gas entering the bed must have a suitable temperature for catalytic reaction. Although reaction heat sustains the catalyst, appropriate preheat is needed for complete and stable reaction. A shell-and-tube exchanger indirectly heats air with hot product gas: nitrogen oxides pass inside the tubes and air outside them.

The described exchanger has about 2 m² heat-transfer area, is 1.4 m long and 0.3 m in diameter, and contains eighteen 25 mm heating pipes. Internal baffles improve heat transfer. Insulate the shell with the asbestos-based paste mentioned in the original. Provide a thermometer pocket in the preheated-air line.

![Air preheater, longitudinal view](../assets/figures/p19_f01.png)

![Air preheater, end view](../assets/figures/p19_f02.png)

### 7. Ammonia pressure-buffer vessel

Ammonia feed is normally supplied as liquid in steel cylinders. Direct cylinder feed is difficult to control and may carry liquid into the system. On meeting hot air, the liquid could expand suddenly and disrupt operation. The text therefore vaporizes ammonia in the cylinder, for example by applying hot water, before sending it into a storage and pressure-buffer vessel.

The iron vessel is to withstand 15 kg/cm² and have an ammonia pressure gauge. Operating pressure is about 4–5 kg/cm². If pressure is too high, remove the hot water from the cylinder to stop vaporization. Control delivery with the outlet valve. Existing equipment may be converted; larger volume provides more reserve and more time to change cylinders.

![Ammonia pressure-buffer vessel](../assets/figures/p20_f01.png)

### 8. Nitrogen-oxide gas cooler

Gas leaving the air preheater is generally about 400°C. An additional heat exchanger can make hot water for ammonia vaporization and reduce gas temperature to about 150–160°C before the cooler. Cooling promotes oxidation of nitric oxide by remaining oxygen to nitrogen dioxide.

If this heat is not recovered, add iron pipe for natural cooling; otherwise the ceramic or glass cooler may be damaged by excessive inlet temperature. Keep the iron pipe outlet above 120°C to avoid corrosion.

Ceramic pipes transfer heat relatively poorly and require greater area. The illustrated cooler uses eleven pipes of 0.1 m internal diameter and 2 m length, providing about 7 m² area. Additional pipes may be added where feasible.

#### Flat-ended ceramic pipes

Connect straight sections and elbows in an S-shaped path. The original uses asbestos-board gaskets first treated in paraffin and coated on both sides with tallow, then clamped with flanges.

![Flat-ended ceramic-pipe connection](../assets/figures/p21_f01.png)

#### Socketed ceramic pipes

Pack the joints with asbestos rope soaked in water glass and seal them with acid-resistant cement or diabase-powder mortar. Allow the joints to dry fully; inadequate sealing causes acid or gas leakage.

![Socketed ceramic-pipe connection](../assets/figures/p21_f02.png)

#### Glass-pipe cooler

China No. 3 Dye Works in Shanghai reported successful glass coolers at lower cost than ceramic pipes. Their arrangement is similar, using straight pipes and elbows. At each joint, wrap three or four layers of PVC cloth coated with PVC adhesive, then cloth coated with perchlorovinyl varnish. Once dry, cover the outside with a rubber sleeve and clamp both ends to the glass. Fix the assembly to a wooden frame.

The glass pipes are about 40 mm in diameter. Three parallel rows of ten pipes give about 10 m² cooling area. The text reports better cooling than ceramic pipes.

![Glass-pipe cooler](../assets/figures/p22_f01.png)

### 9. Oxidation and absorption towers

After cooling, nitric oxide progressively oxidizes to nitrogen dioxide in the oxidation tower. Conversion depends on cooling, tower volume and other factors. Lower temperature and greater volume favour oxidation. Conventional plants sometimes use refrigeration; a locally equipped plant may use a larger volume without circulating cooling, avoiding acid pumps and motors. Circulating cooling improves results where available.

Oxidation releases heat, so tower gas may be hotter than the cooler outlet. At 30°C, the stated conversion is about 25% after four seconds and 75% after one minute.

For 75% conversion at 30°C, the text specifies about 53 seconds residence time and 300 m³/h gas flow, then prints:

$$
\frac{300\times13}{60\times60}=4.4\ \mathrm{m^3}
$$

> Editorial note: the prose gives 53 seconds, the printed numerator gives 13, and the result is 4.4. These are inconsistent. The equation is preserved as printed rather than silently recalculated.

Better cooling permits a smaller volume; the text generally selects 5 m³. The described tower is about 1.0 m in diameter and 6 m high. It may be built of acid-resistant brick, ceramic brick or stone using acid-resistant cement or diabase-powder mortar. Joints must be tight and the tower fully dry before use. Water jars or commercially supplied ceramic tower sections are alternatives; calculate their individual volume and required number first.

#### Towers made of water jars

Use jars of the same dimensions, joining mouth to mouth and bottom to bottom. Seal joints with water-glass-soaked asbestos rope and diabase-powder mortar. Make roughly a dozen holes about 30 mm across in the bottoms for gas passage. A common “seven-shi” jar is stated to hold about 0.4 m³; twelve jars provide sufficient volume. Rather than a dangerously tall stack, use two six-jar towers in series or parallel. Support the jars against tilting and reinforce their mouths with bamboo hoops.

![Oxidation-tower construction](../assets/figures/p24_f01.png)

#### Ceramic-section towers

These are relatively convenient to install, with joints sealed like those of the cooler. Do not impose the weight of the entire tower on the lowest section, as sections may break. Each weighs about 300 kg. Iron bands around every two sections should transfer load to the supporting frame. The number of sections depends on diameter and height.

#### Brick towers

Acid-resistant or ceramic-brick towers take longer to build but permit unrestricted diameter and do not require a separate supporting frame. Water jars are cheaper, reportedly a little over ten yuan each; ceramic and acid-resistant bricks are more expensive. Jar and ceramic towers predominated in Shanghai because they could be installed quickly.

#### Absorption towers

These have similar construction but contain 50 × 50 mm ceramic rings. Nitrogen dioxide is absorbed by liquid on the packing surfaces. Coke or pumice may substitute after thorough washing to remove fine particles that could block pipes. With coke, do not completely fill the tower: insufficient free space reduces further nitric-oxide oxidation.

Circulate liquid with a pump and provide cooling. Where tower height is limited, use two parallel rows. The stated absorption volume is 20 m³ per tonne of 100% nitric acid. Processing 500 kg of ammonia gives about 1.5 tonnes, requiring 30 m³ total, or five towers of 6 m³ each.

Poor cooling, particularly in summer, permits yellow nitrogen oxides to escape from the fifth tower, reducing yield and affecting environmental hygiene. The text proposes improved cooling, reduced throughput, or an alkaline tail-gas tower recovering sodium nitrite.

Install successive towers with a 20–30 cm difference in elevation so acid can overflow from the fifth towards the first; five towers on one level make acid transfer difficult.

![Absorption-tower arrangement](../assets/figures/p25_f01.png)

Commercial ceramic acid distributors are available. Alternatively, place three porcelain cups on the packing and one above them. Pump acid first into the top cup; some overflows directly onto the packing and some into the three lower cups, spreading it more evenly.

The circulating-acid coolers may use glass tubes like the gas cooler. The specified areas are 3 m² for the first tower and 2 m² each for the second and third. The fourth and fifth also have a stated 2 m², though their coolers may be omitted.

Hardwood acid pumps, coated internally six or seven times with perchlorovinyl varnish, were reported to resist acid adequately. Their delivery is about 8 m³/h to a head of 8 m. Wooden pumps from Shanghai Minghuang Works were used; the text anticipates replacing wood with rigid PVC for better acid resistance.
