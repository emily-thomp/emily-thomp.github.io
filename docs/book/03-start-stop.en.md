# Startup and shutdown

## Preparations before startup

1. Check the tightness of all pipe joints. Gas and liquid lines must not leak; seal any leaking points.
2. Check the power supply to the blower, acid pumps and other driven equipment. Protect electrical wiring from acid contact, which could cause current leakage or electric shock.
3. Check thermometers and flowmeters for malfunction, to avoid difficulty in control.
4. Connect the ammonia cylinder to the pressure-buffer vessel before startup and establish a reserve of ammonia. The pressure gauge is to show about 4 kg/cm². Vaporize additional liquid ammonia from the cylinder when operation begins.

## Startup procedure

1. Start the acid-circulation pumps so that liquid circulates in the absorption towers. Check the pump glands for leakage; the gland packing of wooden pumps is liable to leak and requires frequent attention.
2. Start the blower and send a small quantity of air, stated as a flow of about 10 cubic metres, through the system. Preheated air makes catalyst ignition easier. The book describes heating the air pipe downstream of the flowmeter externally with coal to reach 200–250°C.
3. When the contact reactor reaches about 150–200°C, open the ignition opening at the top, normally used as a viewing port, and heat the catalyst with a hydrogen flame. As the indicated thermocouple temperature reaches 300–350°C, introduce ammonia gradually. If the temperature rises slowly, a little ammonia may be admitted to initiate reaction and speed heating.

During ignition, the contact reactor is to be under negative pressure. The book describes an exhaust fan on a branch of the outlet pipe, or a steam ejector where steam is available. This draws the hydrogen flame into the catalyst bed and allows ignition in approximately 10–15 minutes. With substantial positive pressure, the flame may burn above the bed, emerge from the ignition opening, or ignite ammonia; the text emphasizes this point.

At ignition, the stated ammonia concentration is about 10.5%. A dark-red catalyst surface indicates that reaction has begun and ignition may cease. At lower ammonia concentration, the bed may appear red under the hydrogen flame but stop reacting after the flame is withdrawn, with the redness and temperature disappearing.

At normal bed temperature, generally about 700–800°C, adjust ammonia concentration to 10–10.5% and air preheat to about 200°C. A lower ammonia concentration calls for higher air preheat. The red colour of the catalyst surface indicates normal reaction. If the redness fades while thermocouple temperature rises rapidly, the text interprets this as decomposition rather than oxidation, with reduced oxidation efficiency.

Where a single platinum gauze is combined with an iron–bismuth catalyst, take care that the ignition rod does not tear the gauze.

4. After ignition, observe the gradual rise in bed temperature. At the normal temperature—about 700–750°C for a non-platinum catalyst, or about 750°C for a combined platinum/non-platinum bed—increase air and ammonia flows gradually to their specified values. Increase air first, then ammonia, raising both proportionately. Do not increase ammonia alone, which would produce excessive ammonia concentration.
5. Once operation is normal, divert the air through the heat exchanger so that reaction heat preheats it.

## Shutdown procedure

1. Close the ammonia valve and stop ammonia admission. The reaction then stops.
2. After 15 minutes, stop the air blower and close the air valve.
3. Wait a further 10 minutes, stop acid circulation, close the pump inlet and outlet valves, and stop the acid pumps.
4. For an extended shutdown, stop ammonia vaporization in advance and use the stored gas before shutting down.
